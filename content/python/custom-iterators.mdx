<h1 id="why-write-a-custom-iterator-at-all">
  Why Write a Custom Iterator at All
</h1>

Most data containers you reach for in everyday Python already iterate. Lists, tuples, dicts, sets, files, even strings know how to feed values to a `for` loop. The cases where you'd write your own iterator look like this:

- You're walking a custom structure (a category tree, a paginated catalog, an order history) and you want callers to use a plain `for` loop instead of memorizing your traversal API.
- You want results to be **lazy**: produced one at a time, on demand, instead of building a giant list in memory.
- You want to control how the iteration is ordered (reverse chronological, depth-first, oldest first) without exposing the underlying storage.

The mechanics are simple once you've seen them a few times. The design choice between "one class" and "two classes" is the part worth being careful about, and it's the part most lessons gloss over.

Before going further, a quick refresher on the vocabulary:

- An **iterable** is anything you can pass to `iter()`. It has an `__iter__` method that returns a fresh iterator.
- An **iterator** is what `iter()` returns. It has a `__next__` method that yields the next value, or raises `StopIteration` when there's nothing left. By contract, an iterator's own `__iter__` returns `self`.

That distinction (between iterables and iterators) is the whole reason this lesson has two patterns instead of one.
